How to Read a Water Damage Scope of Work

What each section is for, which numbers to check and how the scope connects to your claim.

☎ Call (740) 231-9132

By the Mount Vernon Water Damage Pros team · Published November 10, 2026

A scope of work is the written list of what a contractor will do, where and with what. It is the single most important paper in a water damage job, because your insurer, your contractor and you will all rely on it.

Here is how to read one, section by section, so you know what you are agreeing to. The language can look technical, but the structure is simple once you see it.

The header: who, where and what happened

The top of the scope should name the property, the date of loss, the cause and the water category. If any of those is missing or wrong, ask for a correction, because they affect how your insurer reads the rest.

Check the address and the description of the source against what you know. A scope that names the wrong cause can cause trouble later.

Affected areas and measurements

The scope lists rooms or areas with measurements, often in square feet or linear feet. Compare them to your own sense of the loss, and ask to see the moisture map that supports them.

A scope should not guess. If a number is an estimate, it should say so, and it should be revised when the crew has measured.

Mitigation lines

These lines cover extraction, removal and drying. Look for equipment counts, days and the standard the work follows. The ANSI/IICRC S500 standard is the usual reference.

Each line should say what is being done and where. A line that says only dry out with no detail should be questioned.

Removal and disposal lines

Removed materials are listed with areas, such as drywall cut two feet above the floor in a bedroom. The reason should be clear from the readings. Disposal should be named.

If a material is listed as removed but the readings show it dry, ask why.

Rebuild lines

Rebuild lines list replacement materials and finishes. They may be in the same document or a separate one, and your insurer may require its own estimate. Ask which phase the scope covers.

Pay attention to the finish level, such as texture, paint and trim, since those are common sources of disagreement.

Terms, exclusions and signatures

The back of the scope lists payment terms, exclusions and what happens if conditions change. Read it before you sign, and do not sign anything that assigns your insurance benefits unless you understand it.

A fair scope explains how changes are handled and gets your approval before they are billed. For a wall-heavy loss, the wall and drywall page shows the decisions the lines should reflect.

A checklist to bring to the page

  • Does the header name the right address, date and cause?
  • Do the areas and measurements match what you saw?
  • Is each piece of equipment named with a count and days?
  • Are removal areas tied to moisture readings?
  • Does it say who handles permits, disposal and cleanup?
  • Are changes and extra costs explained in writing?

When the contractor and insurer scopes disagree

It is common for two scopes to differ, since each is written for a different purpose. Ask for both in writing, compare line by line and ask each side to explain the differences.

A difference in a few lines is normal, and a gap in a large item deserves a conversation. Keep the moisture log and photos, since they support whichever side the facts favor.

Mount Vernon note: ask about permits in the scope

The City of Mount Vernon lists permit portals for engineering and code enforcement on its website. Whether your repair needs a permit depends on the work, so ask the city building department, and ask your contractor whether permits are included in the scope or left to you.

If you live in Gambier, check with the village office, and the Gambier page covers the basics. Write down who is responsible for permits before work starts.

Common questions

Who writes the scope?

The contractor usually writes it, and your insurer may write its own. Comparing the two helps catch gaps.

What if the scope changes during the job?

It should be updated in writing with the reason and any price change before the work is billed.

Should I sign right away?

No. Read it, ask questions and take the time you need. A fair contractor will not pressure you.

Can I ask for changes to the scope?

Yes. Ask for the reason behind a line and request a revision in writing if something is wrong or missing.
