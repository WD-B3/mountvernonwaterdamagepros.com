Selling a House With Water Damage: Disclosure Basics

Disclosure basics, the records that help and the choice between repairing and selling as-is.

☎ Call (740) 231-9132

By the Mount Vernon Water Damage Pros team · Published November 10, 2026

Selling a house that has had water damage is common, and it does not have to scare buyers away. What matters is that the damage was handled properly, that you can show the records and that you are honest about what you know.

This guide covers the disclosure basics for sellers in Mount Vernon, OH, the documents that help, and the repair decision. It is general information, so talk to your real estate agent or attorney about your sale.

The disclosure form in Ohio

Under Ohio Revised Code 5302.30, most sellers of one-to-four-unit homes must give buyers a state-prescribed property disclosure form, signed and dated, as soon as is practicable. The law requires good-faith disclosure of material defects within your actual knowledge.

Some transfers are exempt, such as certain estate, foreclosure and family transfers, and the exceptions are listed in the statute. Your agent or attorney can tell you whether yours is one of them. Never hide a problem you know about, because that is how a repair becomes a dispute.

Records that help you and the buyer

  • The restoration company scope and drying log, with moisture readings showing the structure reached dry.
  • Photos of the damage and the repair.
  • Plumber or roofer invoices showing the source was fixed.
  • Permit and inspection records, where a permit applied.
  • Insurance claim paperwork, if you filed one.

A dry log from the time of the loss is the best evidence that the problem was handled. Without it, a buyer has to rely on your word.

Repair first or sell as is

Repairing before you list gives buyers a clean house, and it lets you control the story. Selling as is can save time and money, but expect buyers to ask for inspections and to negotiate the price.

Fixing the source of the water and drying the structure are the steps most buyers care about. Cosmetic repair is less urgent, and you can discuss it in negotiation.

What buyers and inspectors look for

Inspectors use moisture meters and thermal cameras to look for signs of past or present water, such as stains, soft spots and raised readings. Fresh paint over a stain raises questions.

If an inspector finds moisture, a restoration company can check it and explain whether it is old, new or active. The structural drying page explains how readings are used to confirm a structure is dry.

Mold and past leaks

If mold followed the water, document how it was removed and what was done about moisture. The mold remediation page explains the steps and the standard a professional job follows, and buyers often ask for those records.

Be honest about what you know and keep the paperwork organized. Your agent can help present it.

How inspectors and lenders may react

A buyer inspector may find moisture, stains or repairs that a seller has not mentioned. A lender or appraiser may ask about known defects, and unresolved water damage can complicate financing.

Having records that show the source was fixed and the structure was dried is the simplest way to answer questions without drama. Your agent can package them with the listing.

Talking points for showings

  • What happened, when and how it was stopped.
  • What was repaired and by whom, with paperwork available.
  • What the dry log showed at the end.
  • What has been done since, such as new sump pumps or downspout changes.

Keep the story short, factual and consistent with the disclosure form.

Mount Vernon note: the statute, not a rumor

The disclosure duty comes from Ohio Revised Code section 5302.30, which sets out the form, the timing and the exceptions. The text lists items such as the water supply source and sewer system type, the condition of the roof, foundation, walls and floors, and any material defects within the seller actual knowledge.

In Mount Vernon, the city water supply comes from groundwater wells, and a sale in Gambier involves village water that comes from the city, as the Gambier page explains. Whatever your address, give the buyer accurate answers about the source and the repair.

Common questions

Do I have to disclose water damage that was fully repaired?

The statute asks for material defects within your actual knowledge, and your agent or attorney can tell you how past repaired damage should be described on the form. Honesty is the safest route.

Can I sell without repairing?

Yes, but expect inspections and price negotiation. Records of the source repair and the drying help a lot.

Will a buyer ask for a restoration report?

Often. A drying log and a scope of work are the documents that show the loss was handled.

Should I mention insurance claims?

Ask your agent or attorney how claims history should be handled. Keep the paperwork organized either way.
