The Water Damage Remediation Process, Step by Step

A plain walkthrough of what the crew does, in order, and why the order matters.

☎ Call (740) 231-9132

By the Mount Vernon Water Damage Pros team · Published November 10, 2026

The water damage remediation process is a sequence, and each step makes the next one possible. Skipping or reordering steps is how a house that looks dry ends up with a musty smell and a soft floor three weeks later.

Here is the usual order, from the call to the final reading, so you know what to expect and what to ask. The details change with the cause, but the logic holds for most losses.

Step 1: stop the source and make the area safe

Nothing else works while water is still coming in. The first call is about shutting off the main valve, cutting power to wet areas if the panel is dry and keeping people away from hazards. A crew will not start extraction in a room with live electrical danger.

Photos taken now document the loss before anything moves. They are worth more than most people expect when the claim is reviewed.

Step 2: assess the water and map the moisture

The crew identifies the category of water, which the ANSI/IICRC S500 standard defines as clean, gray or contaminated, and the class of damage, which describes how much of the structure absorbed it. A moisture meter and thermal camera map the wet areas, including ones that look dry.

This assessment sets the plan. Clean water caught early may mean drying materials in place, while contaminated water means removal.

Step 3: extract the standing water

Pumps and extraction units remove the bulk of the water. This step is covered in more detail on the water extraction page, and it is the step that most reduces the total damage.

The faster water leaves, the less of it travels into walls and subfloors. That is why a call at 2 a.m. is better than a call at 9 a.m.

Step 4: remove what cannot be saved

Materials such as soaked carpet pad, wet insulation and drywall that has gone soft are removed, because they will not dry fast enough to avoid mold. Removal also opens pathways for air to reach hidden wet areas.

Removed items are photographed and listed, because the list becomes part of the scope and the claim.

Step 5: dry the structure

Air movers and dehumidifiers run until the readings match the dry standard. The structural drying page describes the equipment and the logs.

The crew returns on a schedule to check readings, move equipment and decide when drying is done. Pulling equipment on a fixed date regardless of readings is a warning sign.

Step 6: clean, treat and document

Surfaces left in place are cleaned and sometimes treated, and the crew writes up the drying log, the removed materials and the readings at close. That document is what your insurer and any future buyer will want.

Step 7: rebuild

Rebuilding replaces drywall, flooring and trim, and it is often a separate contract with its own price. It may involve the same company or another, and your insurer may have a view on that.

Ask for the written scope of both stages up front, so you can compare what mitigation covers with what the rebuild will add.

The equipment you will see on site

  • Extraction units and pumps that remove standing water.
  • Air movers that push air across wet surfaces.
  • LGR or desiccant dehumidifiers that pull moisture from the air.
  • Moisture meters and thermal cameras that show what is wet.
  • Air scrubbers with HEPA filters when dust or mold is a concern.

Each piece has a job, and the crew should be able to tell you what it is for and how long it will run.

What you can do to help during the process

Keep the affected rooms closed off, leave the equipment alone and do not turn off dehumidifiers to save noise. Move small valuables out of the work area and tell the crew about anything fragile.

Keep a simple log of dates and what you were told, plus any messages with your insurer. When questions come up later, a clear record saves time for everyone.

Mount Vernon note: why the first hours matter

The City of Mount Vernon supplies drinking water from groundwater, drawn from the Riverside Park well field and a well west of the Kokosing River, according to its water quality report. That means a supply line break begins as clean, Category 1 water, which is the best case for saving materials.

But clean water does not stay clean. Standing water picks up contaminants from floors and soil, and the S500 standard says water can move to a worse category as it sits. In practice, a clean-water loss that is extracted within hours can often be dried, while one left for a day or two may need removal. For a house in nearby Gambier, whose water also comes from the city, the same rule applies, as the Gambier page explains.

Common questions

How long does remediation take?

Extraction takes hours and drying usually takes several days, so most of the timeline is the drying and the rebuild. The readings decide when drying ends.

Do I have to leave my home?

Often not, but heavily affected rooms may be unusable and contaminated water may require leaving. The crew will say what is safe.

What is the difference between category and class?

Category describes how contaminated the water is, and class describes how much of the structure took on moisture. Both shape the plan.

Is it normal for the house to be loud and warm?

Yes. Air movers and dehumidifiers are noisy and add heat, and both are signs that drying is working.
