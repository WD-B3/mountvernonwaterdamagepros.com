What a Water Damage Restoration Estimate Should Include

The lines to expect, the vague ones to question and the numbers that should match your readings.

☎ Call (740) 231-9132

By the Mount Vernon Water Damage Pros team · Published November 10, 2026

A good water damage estimate reads like a plan, not a total. It tells you what will be removed, what equipment will run, for how long and what will be rebuilt, so you can compare it with another estimate or show it to an insurer.

This guide lists what a restoration estimate should include and the vague lines that deserve a question. It does not give prices, because those depend on the loss, but it will help you read whatever number you receive.

The basics every estimate should list

  • The date, the address and a description of the cause and the water category.
  • The areas affected, with measurements or square footage.
  • The moisture readings that support the scope.
  • Whether the estimate covers mitigation, rebuild or both.

Equipment and drying days

The estimate should name the equipment, such as air movers, LGR dehumidifiers and extraction units, with quantity and days. A line that says only drying with no count tells you nothing.

Days should be tied to readings. If the estimate says three days and the structure is not dry, the crew should keep working, and the estimate should explain how an extension is handled.

Materials removed and disposal

Look for a list of what is coming out, such as carpet pad, drywall, insulation or baseboards, with areas. Disposal should be included or priced separately.

Be wary of a scope that removes more than the readings support. Drywall is usually cut to the wet line, and the crew should be able to show you meter readings.

Cleaning, treatment and safety

Contaminated water needs cleaning and disinfecting steps, and the estimate should list them. For sewage or flood water, protective gear and sanitizing are real costs.

If mold is involved, containment and negative air should appear as separate lines. The mold remediation page explains what those include.

Rebuild lines and exclusions

Rebuild lines list new drywall, insulation, flooring, trim and paint, and they are usually a separate stage. Ask what is excluded, such as permits, cabinet work or contents.

An estimate that is clear about exclusions protects you from surprise changes later.

Red flags

  • A single lump sum with no breakdown.
  • Pressure to sign immediately.
  • A promise of insurance approval or direct payment.
  • No mention of readings or a drying log.

What to do with the estimate

Send a copy to your insurer, compare it with a second one if you can and ask questions about any line you do not understand. For a typical wet-carpet loss, the carpet page shows the decisions an estimate should reflect.

A sample read of the lines

Imagine an estimate with a line for water extraction, a line for drywall removal, a line for air movers and dehumidifiers with a count and days, and a line for disposal. Each line should match something you saw and something in the readings.

If you see a line for equipment with no count, ask for one. If you see a removal area larger than the wet area on the moisture map, ask for the readings that support it.

How an estimate changes when hidden damage turns up

Opening a wall can reveal wetter insulation or framing than the surface showed, and the scope may change. A fair company tells you, shows you the reading and gets your approval before billing more.

Ask in advance how changes are handled and whether the price is a fixed amount or based on actual use. Either can be fair, but you should know which before work begins.

Mount Vernon note: contractor fraud is a real concern

The Ohio Department of Insurance includes guidance on recognizing and avoiding contractor fraud in its severe weather and recovery toolkit. Its consumer services office is the place to ask if an estimate or a contractor seems off, and the toolkit is listed at insurance.ohio.gov.

For a Gambier home, the same advice applies, and the Gambier page covers the village. Ask any contractor for the written scope before you sign.

Common questions

Is a verbal estimate enough?

No. Get the scope and price in writing so you and your insurer can compare it to the work performed.

Why do two estimates differ so much?

Often because one includes rebuild, contents or more equipment days than the other. Compare line by line before you compare totals.

Should the estimate change after the work starts?

It can, if hidden damage turns up, but changes should be explained with readings and documented before they are billed.

What if the estimate is lower than my insurer?

Compare the lines. A lower number may leave out equipment days, disposal or rebuild, and your insurer may have its own pricing, so ask what drives the difference.
