Water Damage in a Rental: What Tenants and Landlords Should Know

Reporting, repairs, belongings and records, for both sides of a rental water loss.

☎ Call (740) 231-9132

By the Mount Vernon Water Damage Pros team · Published November 10, 2026

Water damage in a rental involves at least two parties and two insurance policies, and confusion about who does what can slow everything. The tenant usually finds the problem, and the landlord usually owns the repair, so clear communication is the first fix.

This guide covers the steps for tenants and landlords in Mount Vernon and Gambier, OH. It is general information, not legal advice, so ask a lawyer or a local tenant resource about your lease.

What a tenant should do first

  1. Stop the water if you safely can, usually by closing the shutoff at the fixture or the main.
  2. Move your belongings out of the wet area and photograph the damage.
  3. Report the problem to the landlord or manager right away, in writing, and keep a copy.
  4. Call the emergency line if the damage is spreading, since time matters.
  5. Contact your renter insurer about your belongings.

What the landlord owes

Under Ohio Revised Code 5321.04, a landlord must make repairs needed to keep the unit fit and habitable and keep plumbing and other fixtures in good, safe working order. That is why the owner generally arranges the repair of a leaking pipe or a damaged ceiling.

The statute also requires reasonable notice before entry, with 24 hours presumed reasonable, except in an emergency. A water emergency is the kind of case where quick access matters.

Who pays for what

The landlord insurer typically covers the building, and the tenant insurer covers the tenant belongings and sometimes extra living costs. Who caused the damage, and whether it was sudden, shapes the claim.

A tenant who caused the leak may be responsible for the cost, and a leak from an old pipe usually is the owner problem. Written records of when you reported it help everyone.

Documentation that protects both sides

  • Photos and video taken when the problem was found.
  • Written notice with the date, time and how it was sent.
  • A dry log and scope from the restoration company.
  • A list of damaged belongings with approximate values.
  • Receipts for emergency costs such as hotels or repairs.

If the unit is not livable

Severe water damage can make a unit unfit to live in, and the lease and local law decide what happens to rent and a place to stay. Ask a lawyer or tenant resource what your lease and Ohio law provide.

In a multi-unit building, water can travel between units, so the crew checks walls and ceilings next door. For a shared-building loss, the commercial water damage page explains how multi-party buildings are handled.

A sample timeline for a rental water loss

  1. Day one: the tenant finds water, shuts it off if safe and photographs it.
  2. Same day: written notice goes to the landlord, and the emergency line is called.
  3. Day one or two: extraction and drying start, and the owner contacts the insurer.
  4. Days two to seven: drying continues, with readings logged.
  5. Afterward: the owner arranges repairs, and the tenant files for belongings if needed.

If you disagree with the response

If a landlord does not respond, send a second written notice and keep records of both. A tenant resource, legal aid office or local housing office can explain your options.

Landlords who disagree with a tenant about the cause can ask the restoration company for the source documentation. Clear records reduce disputes on both sides.

Mount Vernon note: Gambier has its own mix of renters

Gambier is a small village built around Kenyon College, and many households rent. If you rent in Gambier, report the damage to the owner right away and keep the date. The village maintains water distribution lines and gets its water from the City of Mount Vernon, so a street-side problem is a village matter, and an indoor leak is a landlord matter. The Gambier page covers this in detail.

For Mount Vernon rentals, the same landlord duties apply under Ohio law. The statute, Revised Code 5321.04, is the place to read the duties in full, and a legal aid or tenant resource can explain how it applies to your lease.

Common questions

Can my landlord enter without notice during a leak?

The statute presumes 24 hours of notice is reasonable, and notice is not required in an emergency. A spreading water leak can qualify, and a lawyer can say how it applies to your case.

Does my landlord insurance cover my belongings?

Usually not. A renter policy covers your own belongings, so ask your insurer about it.

Who should call the restoration company?

Either can, but the landlord usually decides on the repair. If the damage is spreading, call right away and tell the owner.

Should a tenant hire a restoration company directly?

Usually the owner decides, but in an emergency you can call to stop the damage and tell the owner right away. Keep records of what you did.
